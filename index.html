How it works

Dewater designs a ring of dewatering wells around an excavation in an unconfined aquifer underlain by an impermeable layer. It is a quick screening tool: steady or transient flow, a uniform isotropic aquifer, fully penetrating wells, and the Dupuit assumptions.

The equation

One well pumping q from an unconfined aquifer lowers the head h at distance r to

H² − h² = (q / πk) · ln(R / r)

The governing equation is linear in h², so several wells add in H² − h², not in drawdown:

h = √( H² − Σ (qi / πk) · ln(R / ri) )

H is the initial saturated thickness, k the hydraulic conductivity, R the radius of influence, and ri the distance from well i. A well has no effect beyond R, and the head at a well is taken at its radius rw.

The minimum design pumping rate

The minimum design pumping rate, Qmin, is the smallest total rate that brings the water down to the required level everywhere inside the excavation. With the total rate Q shared equally by n wells, the head at each point inside the excavation is h = √(H² − (Q/n)·A), where A = Σ ln(R/ri)/(πk) depends only on where the wells are. The highest head is at the point with the smallest A, so Qmin is exact:

Qmin = n (H² − htarget²) / min A

Points are checked on a grid over the excavation and along its edge, where the water is usually highest. A design fails if Qmin would pump a well dry (the head at its radius drops to zero): add wells or move them out. The rate actually specified is usually Qmin plus a margin.

Transient pumping

Steady flow needs R, the least certain input. Transient flow replaces it with the specific yield Sy and the time t since the wells started pumping. Each well follows the Theis solution in the same h² form, with the transmissivity kH:

H² − h² = (q / 2πk) · W(u),    u = r² Sy / (4kHt)

W(u) is the Theis well function. At late time W(u)/2 → ln(1.5√(kHt/Sy)/r) (Cooper–Jacob), so the steady equation holds with R = 1.5√(kHt/Sy), growing with time. The equation is still linear in q, so the wells add as before and Qmin is still exact: the smallest rate that brings the water to the required level everywhere in the excavation by day t. The pumping follows one schedule, in two phases, adjusted once a week (rate changes add in time, as in a recovery test). Drawdown: pump Q, cut back only as far as needed to keep every well from going dry; the wells can take much more at the start than later, as the cone deepens. Hold: once the excavation is dewatered, step down to the lowest rate that holds the water at the required level for the next four weeks. Find Qmin gives the smallest starting rate that meets the deadline (the constant rate, when the wells can deliver it), or, if the wells can't meet it, the fastest they can do and the day it takes. Pumping schedule plots the steps and gives the water pumped. The results give the day the excavation is dewatered, the bar under the map plays the contours and the section through time (up to twice the design day). The design curves plot the starting rate of the deadline design against the number of wells (marking the fewest that can meet the deadline), against k (what a wrong guess at k costs), and against the deadline, with the water pumped to dewater (marking the shortest deadline the wells can meet). Sy is taken as constant (no delayed yield at early times), and the drawdown should stay modest compared with H. A recharge boundary such as a river, or the edge of the aquifer, ends the growth; a steady R stands for that case.

A cutoff wall

A slurry cutoff wall around the site, keyed into the low-k layer, cuts off the inflow: water reaches the inside only through the wall. Placed around the site, the wall is a rectangle set out by the setback from the excavation and the wells, so it encloses both. With a wall the aquifer is no longer uniform, so the wells' influence comes from a finite-difference model instead of the Theis solution, in the same h² form:

(Sy/H) ∂u/∂t = ∇·(k ∇u) + 2q at the wells,   u = H² − h²

The grid is fine over the wall and the wells and grows outward to a fixed head beyond the drawdown's reach (in steady flow, at R). The wall is a barrier on the cell faces it crosses: its thickness t at kw in series with the soil, the trench equation across it. Each well sits at the center of its cell, and the head at its screen adds the well's own cone below the cell's size, so wells can still go dry. The equation is linear in the rates, so the response to the wells pumping is worked out once for each layout and any schedule adds it up in time, as with Theis: the same drawdown, cut-back, and hold, the same Find Qmin, and the same design curves (each point its own grid). With no wall, the grid reproduces the Theis solution to within a percent or two. With a tight wall, the water inside comes mostly from storage, and once it is down the wells need little more than the leakage through the wall. In practice, defects in the wall and a poor key leak more than the wall itself, so allow a margin. A wall that doesn't reach a low-k layer, or a low-k layer that leaks, needs a model with flow under the wall.

The radius of influence in practice

R is the least certain input. When a pumping test is available, R comes from it: plot the drawdowns at the observation wells against the log of distance and extend the line to zero drawdown. Without one, an empirical formula gives an estimate. For a group of wells, the ring is treated as one large well of radius re = √(A/π), where A is the excavation's area, drawn down by s, the drawdown the excavation needs, and R is measured from its center: R = re + 3000 s √k (Sichardt) or re + 575 s √(Hk) (Kusakin), in meters with k in m/s. The pumping rate depends on R only through ln(R/r), so a common check is to run the design for a range of R. R also grows with pumping time (about 1.5√(Tt/S) by the Cooper–Jacob approximation), so a steady R stands for some period of pumping. Because a ring around a compact excavation acts like one large well, Qmin for a small site barely changes with the number of wells.

Steps

  1. Find the site (search, or zoom and pan) and draw the excavation outline, drag a rectangle (Rotate turns it to match the site), or load a GeoJSON polygon.
  2. Enter the site, either as depths below the ground (to the water table, to the top of the low-k layer, and of the excavation, plus any extra depth the water must reach below the excavation bottom) or as elevations (the water table, the top of the low-k layer, the excavation bottom, and the required water level). With depths, the ground elevation is optional; with it, results are also given as elevations. With elevations, it is required. Use terrain fills it with the average ground over the excavation, from open terrain data (USGS 3DEP in the US), and the cross section then shows the actual ground profile. The ground's range under the excavation is listed under the field, Ground contours on the map draws dashed ground contours when zoomed in to building scale, and the corner of the map shows the ground elevation under the pointer. The calculation treats the ground and the water table as level. Then enter k, and choose the flow: steady, with R typed in or from Sichardt's or Kusakin's formula, or transient, with the specific yield and the days to dewater.
  3. Click to place wells, or generate a ring at an offset from the edge.
  4. Optionally, enclose the site with a cutoff wall (a slurry trench): place the wall around the site (a rectangle set out by the setback from the excavation and the wells, so it encloses both), or draw it (a rectangle or a polygon) or load it from GeoJSON, and move its corners where the site allows; then give its thickness and conductivity.
  5. Enter a pumping rate and check the results, or find Qmin.
  6. Choose what the contours show: depth to water, water elevation (with a ground elevation), drawdown, water below the required level, or head above the low-k layer. The orange contour is the required level, and the area where the water is below it is shaded green.
  7. Check a cross section: east–west or north–south through the excavation, or any line you draw. It shows the water level against the static level and the required level, with the ground, the excavation, and the wells on the line.
  8. Use the design curves: Qmin against the number of wells (rings at the offset), against k for the wells on the map (or for the wall), or against the days to dewater (transient flow, or the wall). In transient flow each point is the design for the deadline: the starting rate, cut back weekly where a well would otherwise go dry.

Units: lengths are in meters or feet (the switch at the top of the panel); k and Q each have their own unit, and every flow rate in the results, the design curve, and the exports is given in Q's unit. Changing a unit converts the values already entered.
