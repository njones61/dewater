How it works

Dewater designs a ring of dewatering wells around an excavation in an unconfined aquifer underlain by an impermeable layer. It is a quick screening tool: steady state, a uniform isotropic aquifer, fully penetrating wells, and the Dupuit assumptions.

The equation

One well pumping q from an unconfined aquifer lowers the head h at distance r to

H² − h² = (q / πk) · ln(R / r)

The governing equation is linear in h², so several wells add in H² − h², not in drawdown:

h = √( H² − Σ (qi / πk) · ln(R / ri) )

H is the initial saturated thickness, k the hydraulic conductivity, R the radius of influence, and ri the distance from well i. A well has no effect beyond R, and the head at a well is taken at its radius rw.

The least pumping rate

With the total rate Q shared equally by n wells, the head at each point inside the excavation is h = √(H² − (Q/n)·A), where A = Σ ln(R/ri)/(πk) depends only on where the wells are. The highest head is at the point with the least A, so the least Q that brings every point down to the target head is exact:

Q = n (H² − htarget²) / min A

Points are checked on a grid over the excavation and along its edge, where the water is usually highest. A design fails if that Q would pump a well dry (the head at its radius drops to zero): add wells or move them out.

The radius of influence in practice

R is the least certain input. When a pumping test is available, R comes from it: plot the drawdowns at the observation wells against the log of distance and extend the line to zero drawdown. Without one, an empirical formula such as Sichardt's gives an estimate, usually computed once from the drawdown the design calls for. For a group of wells, the app treats the ring as one large well of radius re = √(A/π), where A is the excavation's area, and takes R = re + 3000 s √k with s the drawdown the excavation needs. The pumping rate depends on R only through ln(R/r), so a common check is to run the design for a range of R. R also grows with pumping time (about 1.5√(Tt/S) by the Cooper–Jacob approximation), so a steady R stands for some period of pumping. Around a compact excavation, a ring of wells behaves much like one large well of radius √(A/π); that is why, for a small site, the least total rate barely changes with the number of wells.

Steps

  1. Find the site (search, or zoom and pan) and draw the excavation outline, drag a rectangle (Rotate turns it to match the site), or load a GeoJSON polygon.
  2. Enter the site: the depths to the water table, to the bottom of the aquifer (the top of the low-k layer), and of the excavation, and how far below the excavation bottom the water must be. Or switch to elevations: the water table, the top of the low-k layer, the excavation bottom, and the required water level, with the ground elevation typed in or read from terrain. A ground elevation is optional; with it, results are also given as elevations. Use terrain fills it with the average ground elevation over the excavation, from open terrain data (USGS 3DEP in the US), and the cross section then shows the actual ground profile. The calculation still treats the ground and the water table as level.
  3. Click to place wells, or generate a ring at an offset from the edge.
  4. Enter a pumping rate and check the contours, or find the least rate that works.
  5. Check a cross section: east–west or north–south through the excavation, or any line you draw. It shows the water level against the static level and the target, with the excavation and the wells on the line.
  6. Use the design curve to choose the number of wells.

Units: lengths are in meters or feet (the switch at the top of the panel); k and Q each have their own unit, and every flow rate in the results, the design curve, and the exports is given in Q's unit. Changing a unit converts the values already entered.

The map shows head, drawdown, or head below the target as contours, and shades the area where the head is below the target. Save the project to come back to it; the file holds the outline, the wells, and the inputs.
